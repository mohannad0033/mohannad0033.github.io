CASE STUDY

Academic security architecture

Smart System for Managing User Permissions in Enterprise Environments

Combining job roles with request context to explain who should receive access, when and why.

My contribution

Team leader in a three-member project team; model comparison, use cases and design documentation.

Read the case study

01

The problem

A job role alone may allow access in situations that should require additional checks. A flexible permission design must account for both responsibility and context, without giving users more access than their task requires.

02

Evidence I analyzed

  • The project compared role-based access control (RBAC) and attribute-based access control (ABAC), including their administration challenges.
  • Representative scenarios covered HR records, working hours, company devices and a manager using company Wi-Fi.
  • The presentation documented a hybrid policy flow, intended benefits, limitations and future extensions.
03

What I did

  1. Compare how roles and attributes make authorization decisions.
  2. Define representative users, resources, actions and contextual conditions.
  3. Model a decision that requires the role permission and the contextual conditions to match.
  4. Lead the team in documenting scenarios, architectural reasoning and policy-management challenges.
04

Why this approach

I combined RBAC's understandable role baseline with ABAC's contextual checks. In the manager example, the role is necessary but company-network access is also required. The tradeoff is greater policy and attribute-management complexity; the design therefore needs reliable attributes and clear rules before implementation.

05

The result

The team produced a documented hybrid permission architecture and representative permit/deny use cases. The output explains authorization logic and least-privilege reasoning; it is a design deliverable, not a production access-control service or evidence of reduced security incidents.

06

Practical impact

Without this approach

In the example, a manager's role could be treated as sufficient even when the connection comes from outside the required network.

With this approach

The proposed policy makes both checks explicit: the user must be a manager and meet the network condition. In practice, this gives organizations a clearer way to express when access should be granted or refused.

Policy examples illustrate the design. A live system would need implementation, reliable identity and attribute sources, testing and ongoing governance.

04 / BENEFITS & CHALLENGES

Flexibility needs careful governance.

Intended benefits

  • Limit inappropriate access to enterprise resources.
  • Combine clear roles with situational authorization.
  • Support least-privilege permission design.
  • Make access requirements easier to explain and document.

Design challenges

  • Define reliable roles, attributes, and policies.
  • Keep permission administration manageable.
  • Account for systems with limited policy support.
  • Maintain accuracy as roles and conditions change.
